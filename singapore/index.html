LMGB · Location analytics · Singapore

Singapore for first-time visitors

Singapore is a city-state of about 6 million people on 735 km². Around 77% of resident households live in HDB flats: public housing built by the Housing & Development Board, sold on 99-year leases and then traded freely on a resale market. Home ownership is above 90%. HDB towns are planned around MRT stations, hawker centres, schools and parks, and every resale transaction is published, which makes Singapore a clean laboratory for asking what drives price.

What you need to know about the place

HDBHousing & Development Board. “HDB block” is one residential building; the data has 8,047 blocks with at least one resale in the last 12 months.
Resale vs BTOBTO (Build-To-Order) flats are bought new from HDB; resale flats are bought from owners at market price. The data covers resale only.
psmPrice per square metre in Singapore dollars (S$). Comparing per m² removes flat-size differences.
LeaseFlats are sold on 99-year leases; older blocks have less lease left, which pulls prices down.
Town vs planning areaHDB towns (Tampines, Bishan…) are housing estates; URA’s 55 planning areas are the official planning units on the map.
MRT · LRTMass Rapid Transit (heavy rail) and Light Rail Transit (feeders in Punggol, Sengkang and Bukit Panjang).
Hawker centreA government-run food court of small cooked-food stalls, planned so every estate has one nearby.
CBDCentral Business District around Raffles Place: the reference point for distance to the city centre.

The idea · recap

Singapore · Lab 2

Neighbours and spatial lag

Each HDB block’s neighbours are its 8 nearest blocks. Lag = their average price.

The idea · temporal lag

Shift daily sales by one day and lay the copy beside the original: that copy is the lag, yt−1. If today resembles yesterday the two move together: autocorrelation. Time has one direction and one obvious neighbour.

The idea · neighbours

Space has no single “yesterday”, so we must define neighbours. Rook: share an edge (4 in a grid). Queen: share an edge or corner (8). Hexagons: exactly 6. For points, a distance band (everyone within d) or the k nearest.

The idea · weights and spatial lag

Four areas A = 10, B = 2, C = 6, D = 8 in a 2 × 2 grid. Rook neighbours: A–B, A–C, B–D, C–D. Write 1 for neighbours, divide each row by its count (row-standardise): that table is W.

lag(y)ᵢ = Σⱼ wᵢⱼ yⱼ

Lag of A = (2 + 6) ÷ 2 = 4; B = (10 + 8) ÷ 2 = 9; C = 9; D = 4. High A sits in a low neighbourhood: a checkerboard.

Singapore · Lab 3

Is the pattern random?

Are hawker centres clustered, spread out, or random across the island?

The idea · randomness

Complete spatial randomness (CSR) is the benchmark: points ignore each other and every spot is equally likely. Clustered points attract (dining strips); dispersed points repel (franchise territories, planned services).

Clustered R < 1

Random R ≈ 1

Dispersed R > 1

The idea · R and Ripley’s L

R = mean nearest distance ÷ 0.5/√(n/A)

R < 1 clustered, R > 1 dispersed. Ripley’s L repeats the test with circles of growing radius; the grey band from 19 random patterns shows what chance gives.

Hawker centres: 803 m vs 907 m, R = 0.89.

Singapore · Lab 4

Autocorrelation and hotspots

Where are prices significantly high or low, given their neighbours?

The idea · Moran’s I

Correlate a map with its own spatial lag. With z = y − ȳ:

I = Σ zᵢ(Wz)ᵢ ÷ Σ zᵢ²

It is the slope of z against its lag. 2 × 2 example: z = (3.5, −4.5, −0.5, 1.5), Wz = (−2.5, 2.5, 2.5, −2.5), so I = −25 ÷ 35 = −0.71, a checkerboard. Shuffle values 999 times; if the real I beats all, p = 0.001.

The idea · LISA and Gi*

LISA keeps each place’s own term zᵢ(Wz)ᵢ and labels it High-High (hotspot), Low-Low, High-Low or Low-High. Gi* compares the total in a window around each place with chance and returns a z-score (1.96 = 95%).

Clusters for territories

The idea · DBSCAN

Two business numbers: ε (how close is “the same place”) and minPts (how many make a market). A point with minPts others within ε is a core; points near a core are border; the rest are noise. Clusters take any shape and their number comes from the data.

Singapore · Lab 6

From samples to surfaces

A continuous price surface from block-level sales.

The idea · IDW

Weight each sample by 1 ÷ distancep. Samples 12, 10, 10 at 350, 750, 850 m give 11.1 with p = 1 and 11.4 with p = 2: higher power trusts the nearest sample more.

The idea · variogram and kriging

Plot half the squared difference of every pair against distance. Nugget: noise at zero distance. Sill: the plateau. Range: where similarity fades out (about 12.8 km for HDB price). Kriging uses this curve to weight samples and returns a standard error for every cell.

Singapore · Lab 7

What explains resale prices?

Does price per m² rise near MRT, hawker centres and the CBD, and is that effect the same island-wide?

The idea · OLS and its residuals

OLS gives one coefficient per variable and assumes independent errors. Map the residuals and test them with Moran’s I. Singapore: R² 0.69, residual I = 0.653.

The idea · lag, error and GWR

Lag: y = ρWy + Xβ + ε

Error: y = Xβ + u, u = λWu + ε

ρ is spillover; λ is shared unmeasured factors. Compare AIC (lower wins). GWR fits a local regression at every point with a kernel that fades to zero at the bandwidth, chosen by AICc. Bandwidth 35 blocks, R² 0.92.

Final product: a one-page decision brief

Question. A buyer with a fixed budget wants the best-value HDB block.

Evidence. Price per m² is strongly spatial (Moran’s I = 0.711). OLS on MRT, hawker and CBD distance fits well (R² 0.69) but leaves clustered residuals (I = 0.653). The spatial error model (λ = 0.85) beats OLS on AIC; GWR reaches R² 0.92 with an MRT effect that changes by town.

Decision. Look at Low-High blocks inside High-High estates, in towns where GWR shows MRT distance matters least, and check remaining lease before shortlisting.
