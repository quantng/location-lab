Start with the decision, not the data

Before opening a spreadsheet, write down three things: who will listen, what they must decide, and what you want them to do. Everything after this, every chart and every title, serves that decision.

Edit the brief. It is kept while this page is open and is used again in step 9, the Big Idea.

Know your data

Gurugram district is cut into 1,646 equal hexagons, each about 0.74 km², roughly the size of 100 football pitches. One row is one hexagon: how many buildings, shops and branded cafés it holds, and how far it is from a metro station.

Sources

Buildings, shops and eateries, and branded cafés: Overture Maps (release October 2026). Built-up land: ESA WorldCover 2021; newly built land: change 2017 to 2024. Metro stations and cafés: OpenStreetMap. Area names are approximate. Shop and café counts are what the maps know about, not a census: say so on the slide.

Rank it: find the extremes

Sorting is the cheapest insight there is. Put the hexagons in order and compare them with the average hexagon: the story is at the top of the list, and in how far the top is from the rest.

Measure

Counts or rates? Two stories

A count tells you where the most is; a rate tells you where it is most intense for its size. Both are true. Choose the one that matches the decision, and say which one you used.

Compare with a benchmark

A number means little alone. Is 55% of cafés near the metro a lot? Only next to something: here, each zone's share of land, buildings and shops. When the bars do not match, there is a story.

Look for relationships

Do two things move together? Put one on each axis. Each dot is a hexagon. The line shows the trend; r measures how tight the link is, from −1 to +1. The dots far below the line are often the opportunity.

A colour map puts every hexagon into a few groups. Where you draw the lines between groups is a choice. With very uneven numbers like shops, one choice makes Gurugram look empty and another makes it look busy everywhere.

Choose the chart for the question

The question decides the chart. Here the question is fixed: which areas have the most branded cafés? Try five ways of answering it and see which one answers at a glance.

Declutter and focus

Every line, colour and word on a chart asks for attention. Take away what does not help, then spend colour only on the point you want remembered. Same data, five stages.

Stage

One sentence: the Big Idea

If the CEO remembers one sentence, what should it be? Join your brief to one insight. The sentence names the audience, states the point and asks for the decision.

Shape the story: setup, tension, resolution

Good stories move. The setup says what everyone already accepts; the tension shows what is wrong with that picture; the resolution says what to do. One sentence carries it: And, But, Therefore.
