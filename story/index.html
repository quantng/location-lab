Start with the decision, not the data

Before opening a spreadsheet, write down three things: who will listen, what they must decide, and what you want them to do. Everything after this, every chart and every title, serves that decision.

Edit the brief. It is kept while this page is open and is used again in step 9, the Big Idea.

Know your data

One row is one municipal ward: an area the Brihanmumbai Municipal Corporation runs as a unit. Mumbai has 24. Know where the numbers come from and how old they are before you build a story on them.

Source

Census of India 2011, published ward by ward by the MCGM (BMC) Public Health Department. Ward boundaries: DataMeet. These numbers are from 2011, so say so on every slide; the next Census will change them.

Rank it: find the extremes

Sorting is the cheapest insight there is. Put the wards in order and compare them with the city average: the story is usually at the top and the bottom of the list.

Counts or rates? Two stories

A count tells you where the most people are; a rate tells you where the problem is most intense. Both are true. Choose the one that matches the decision, and say which one you used.

Compare with a benchmark

A number means little alone. Is 61% high? Only next to something: the city average, last year, a target. Here the three parts of Mumbai are set against the city as a whole.

Look for relationships

Do two things move together? Put one on each axis. Each circle is a ward, sized by its population. The line shows the trend; r measures how tight the link is, from −1 to +1.

Choose the chart for the question

The question decides the chart. Here the question is fixed: which wards have the most people in slums? Try five ways of answering it and see which one answers at a glance.

Declutter and focus

Every line, colour and word on a chart asks for attention. Take away what does not help, then spend colour only on the point you want remembered. Same data, five stages.

Stage

One sentence: the Big Idea

If the Commissioner remembers one sentence, what should it be? Join your brief to one insight. The sentence names the audience, states the point and asks for the decision.

Shape the story: setup, tension, resolution

Good stories move. The setup says what everyone already accepts; the tension shows what is wrong with that picture; the resolution says what to do. One sentence carries it: And, But, Therefore.
